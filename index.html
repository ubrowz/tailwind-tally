1. The short version

Tailwind Tally takes a GPX route and answers two questions:

  • How much of the route is with me or against me? Every 25 metre stretch is a tailwind, headwind or crosswind, with a rough effort figure (sections 3 and 4). To see the other way round, press Reverse route in the Load a route card. The wind is one steady value you set, or a forecast that changes with the clock and the place (sections 2 and 5).
  • How long will it take? At a steady speed the ride time is simply distance over speed. If you ride at steady power instead, your speed drops in a headwind and rises with a tailwind, and the app works out the resulting ride time on a flat road (section 6).

It is a guide, not a measurement. The models were built for typical conditions and a typical rider. Effort, terrain and the individual all matter, and not all of them are in the model. Each section says exactly what is and is not included.

2. What goes in

  • Your speed: the cruising speed you enter, held constant (or, with steady power, your speed on a calm, flat day, plus your weight and the drag and rolling resistance of you and your bike under Advanced; section 6). The app does not model hills, stops or descents.
  • The ride’s start, in forecast mode: the date and the start time. Everything else about timing follows from the route and your speed.
  • The wind: direction and speed, typed in (speed as a Beaufort force) or taken from the forecast.
  • The route: your GPX track, resampled to 25 metre stretches, each with a heading.

Units

Speed and distance use one setting, metric or US, shown in Your ride above the tabs. It starts from the route’s own start point (a simple box around the United States, checked on your device, with no location service asked), and switches automatically when you load a different route — until you pick one yourself, which then sticks for the rest of the visit. Rider mass, drag area and rolling resistance under Advanced stay in kilograms and square metres either way, since they describe your equipment rather than the ride’s result.

What leaves your device

Your GPX file is read in your browser and never uploaded. One optional feature contacts a service, and only when you ask: the forecast sends up to three places from the route, each rounded to about 11 km, and the date, to Open-Meteo. The map tiles are requested as you look at the map. The privacy policy lists all of it.

The forecast

In forecast mode the app asks Open-Meteo for the hourly wind, for the date you pick. It sends up to three places from your route (the start, the middle and the end), each rounded to 0.1° (about 11 km), never the route itself. You give only a start time. The ride then lasts as long as the route takes at your cruising speed, and each stretch gets its own clock time:

time at a stretch = start time + distance ridden so far / your speed

Wind speed is read from the hourly forecast at that moment (linearly between the hours) and direction turns along the shortest arc, and between the three places (linearly by distance along the route). When you press Reverse route, the same places are reached in the other order and at other moments, so the wind you meet can be quite different.

The forecast wind is the standard 10 metre wind, which is also how the Beaufort scale is defined, so manual and forecast wind are directly comparable. If you type the wind yourself it is one steady value for the whole ride.

3. The wind on you

What cools or heats you is not the wind alone but the air moving past you, which is your own motion combined with the wind. On each 25 metre stretch, with v your speed, w the wind speed and φ the angle between the direction you ride and the direction the wind blows toward:

airspeed² = v² + w² − 2 · v · w · cos φ cos φ = +1 pure tailwind airspeed = v − w cos φ = 0 pure crosswind airspeed = √(v² + w²) cos φ = −1 pure headwind airspeed = v + w

Example. You ride at 25 km/h in a Beaufort 4 wind (24 km/h). Straight into it the air passes you at 49 km/h, across it at about 35 km/h, and with it at just 1 km/h. That difference is why the same route feels so different from one stretch to the next.

4. Tailwind, headwind and effort

With the wind on each stretch known (section 3), the app answers two questions: how much of the route is with you or against you, and how much harder the wind makes the ride.

Tailwind, headwind, crosswind

Each stretch is classified by the angle between your heading and the direction the wind blows toward. If the wind pushes along your direction of travel (cos φ > 0) the stretch is easy; if it pushes against you (cos φ < 0) it is hard. A narrow band around exact crosswind, 10° wide by default, counts as neither (neutral). The route map splits the two sides once more: stretches within 20° of dead-on (a 40° band by default) count as full tailwind or headwind. These are plain distance counts: a metre of gentle tailwind counts the same as a metre of gale.

Effort

Air resistance grows with the square of the airspeed, not in proportion to the wind. On a stretch the drag effort is proportional to the airspeed squared (the formula in section 3). The effort estimate is the distance-weighted average of that over the route, divided by the value for riding the same route at the same speed on a calm day. So 1.0× means the same as a calm day and 1.6× means 60 % more aerodynamic effort.

Because of the square, a headwind costs more than an equal tailwind saves. At 25 km/h in a 20 km/h wind, the airspeed is 45 km/h against (3.24× the calm effort) and 5 km/h with (0.04×). A route that is half headwind and half tailwind therefore averages 1.64×, more than a calm day. The estimate counts drag only: not rolling resistance, gradient or changes of speed, and it assumes you hold your cruising speed whatever the wind. If you ride at steady power, the effort figure is replaced by ride times (section 6).

The last half, reversing, and the sweep

The app analyses the route in the direction it is now: the first point is the start (the blue dot on the map). The last 50 % by distance is shown separately, since it is often the part that decides how a ride feels. Reverse route flips the route, so the start moves to the other end and everything on the page is worked out for the other way round; pressing it again restores the original. The app does not compare the two directions for you: press the button and look. Whatever you have fetched stays valid, because the same places are met from the other end: the forecast is turned around with the route, without asking the service again.

The wind-direction sweep chart tries every steady wind direction from 0° to 359° and shows the tailwind share for each; it is a what-if for a steady wind and does not use the forecast. With one steady wind, the app also checks its own arithmetic: the push of the wind along the route must add up to what the gap between the start and the finish allows (the balance check in the full report).

5. A wind that changes during the ride

In forecast mode the wind follows the clock and the place: each stretch is judged against the wind expected when you would be there. Between the hourly values, wind speed changes in a straight line and wind direction turns along the shortest arc: 350° to 10° passes through north, not through south. (Interpolating the east and north components instead would make the wind seem to weaken whenever it turns.) Between the places along the route the same rule applies.

What follows from it. The tailwind and headwind classes and the effort estimate use the wind on each stretch. What stops applying is the balance check of section 4: it assumes one steady wind, so the app shows it as not applicable. Reversing the route is not the same as flipping the signs of the result, because the other direction meets the wind at other moments; the app simply works the new direction out again.

The average. The compass and the Beaufort field show the ride’s average: the mean speed, and a speed-weighted vector mean direction. If the wind changes so much that the average cancels itself out (west for an hour, then east), no single direction describes the ride, and the app says so instead of showing an invented one. Typing or clicking a wind by hand replaces the forecast wind with your steady value.

Seeing it. In forecast mode the Wind card shows a During the ride chart. The horizontal axis is the time of day, with a row of arrows pointing where the wind blows toward (so a north wind points down), above the wind speed. Moving the pointer over the chart, or touching it, shows the values at that moment and puts an orange marker on the route map, where you would be then. With steady power the horizontal axis follows your real speed. The chart appears only when the wind comes from the forecast; a value you type is the same all ride, so there is nothing to chart.

How much it matters. Real forecasts vary. For a Dutch location over 65 days in summer, the wind direction turned by more than 30° during a four-hour window on 28 % of days and by more than 45° on 14 %, though the biggest turns tended to come at light winds. On the 25.7 km sample loop, on a typical day with the wind turning 8° an hour, the time-aware answer differs from a single average wind by no more than about 2 percentage points of tailwind. When a front passes (wind turning 35° an hour, ridden at 10 km/h for 2.6 hours), the difference is 6 to 14 points, enough to change which way round looks better when you compare the two with Reverse route: one average wind says the reversed loop has 4.8 points more tailwind, while the wind at the moment says the loop as drawn has 3.7 points more. So it is a modest refinement on most days and a real one on days when the weather changes, and on long rides.

6. Riding at steady power

By default the app assumes you hold a steady speed. Some riders hold a steady effort instead: their power stays constant, so their speed drops in a headwind and rises with a tailwind. Choose Steady power under Riding style (in the Your ride card, above the tabs, so it applies everywhere) to model that. The speed you enter then means “the speed at this effort on a calm, flat day”, so you do not need to know your watts: the app works out the matching power and shows it.

The power balance

On a flat road, the power at the pedals balances air resistance and rolling resistance:

P · η = ( ½ · ρ · CdA · |vair| · (v − walong) + Crr · m · g ) · v

v is your speed, walong the wind component along your direction of travel (a tailwind is positive), and |vair| the size of the air velocity relative to you, so a crosswind adds drag through its size. ρ is the air density (dry air at sea level, a fixed value), CdA your drag area, Crr the rolling resistance, m the mass of rider and bike, g gravity and η the drivetrain efficiency. The defaults are 85 kg, CdA 0.32 m² (roughly hands on the hoods), Crr 0.005 and η 0.975; you can change them under Advanced.

Calibration

For your entered speed on a calm day, the balance gives the power. At 25 km/h the air resistance takes about 64 W and rolling resistance about 29 W at the wheel, so roughly 96 W at the pedals. That is the steady power for the whole ride.

Solving for the speed, stretch by stretch

On each 25 metre stretch the app finds the speed at which that power exactly balances the resistance in the wind on that stretch. The speed on a stretch depends on the wind when you get there, and that moment depends on how fast you rode the stretches before it, so the ride is worked out stretch by stretch with the clock running at your real speed. In a tailwind the wind itself pushes the bike, so the search for the balancing speed starts beyond the point where the wind alone would carry you.

A headwind costs more speed than an equal tailwind gives back in time. On the 25.7 km sample loop, a calm day at 25 km/h takes 61.8 minutes. Holding the same power in a steady wind from the south-west it takes 63.6 minutes at 10 km/h, 69.1 at 20 km/h (average 22.4 km/h, ranging from 15 to 38 km/h along the loop) and 77.9 at 30 km/h.

What it changes in the results

  • The tailwind and headwind classes depend only on direction, so with a steady wind they are the same as at a steady speed.
  • The effort multiple (section 4) assumes a constant speed, so it is replaced by ride time: for your ride and on a calm day, with the average, slowest and fastest speeds. It also shows the share of the time spent in a headwind, which differs from the share of the distance because you are slower there. In a Beaufort 4 wind the sample loop has 67 % of the time but only 52 % of the distance into a headwind.
  • With a forecast, the wind is read at the moment you really reach each stretch. The average wind shown in the compass fields is found with the calm-day timing, which is close but not exact.

Limits

  • Flat road only. On real routes hills dominate speed, and a rider holding constant power slows far more on a climb than in any wind. Elevation is not used in this calculation, so this shows what the wind does, not the whole ride.
  • Constant power is an idealisation. Many riders hold a perceived effort and vary it with terrain, traffic and fatigue.
  • Not modelled: drafting, cornering, braking, coasting downhill, a changing drag area with position or wind angle, and a changing efficiency. Air density is fixed and ignores temperature, humidity and altitude, which change it by a few per cent.
  • The result depends on the rider parameters. The defaults are typical, not yours.

7. What this is not

  • Not a measurement. It is a model of a typical rider. Individuals differ a lot, and what a route actually feels like is subjective.
  • No effort or terrain. The models do not know how hard you work or what the road looks like. Hills, stops and sprints are not modelled.
  • Constant speed, or constant power on a flat road. The clock and the airspeed use your cruising speed, or with steady power the speed that power gives on each stretch. Climbs, descents, stops and sprints are not modelled, so timing is approximate. The forecast is hourly, so that is usually accurate enough.
  • Wind at 10 metres, hourly. The forecast wind is the standard 10 metre wind, and the wind at a rider’s height is lower, especially among trees and buildings. Gusts are not modelled. Between the hourly values the wind is interpolated, so a sudden shift within an hour is smoothed.
  • Forecast resolution. Weather models resolve places a few kilometres apart at best, and three sample places are used. Local effects such as valleys, coasts, buildings and tree cover are not captured.
  • Not medical or safety advice. Use your own judgement and local guidance, especially in strong wind.

8. How it is checked

The maths is covered by automated tests (in the tests folder of the repository, run with node tests/pure.test.js) that run against the code in this app itself: it is cut out of the page and run as it is. For the wind: directions turn along the shortest arc, a forecast wind that does not change gives the same answer as a steady one, and a wind that reverses halfway along a route is judged stretch by stretch. For steady power: the speed solver returns your entered speed on a calm day, satisfies the power balance exactly on every stretch (headwind, tailwind and crosswind), and matches an independent calculation on the sample loop, and the clock follows the rider’s real speed. The steady-wind results were also compared with a stored set of 60 scenarios before and after the changing-wind work, and were identical. For reversing the route: the fetched places and the forecast are turned around exactly and turning them twice gives back the original, and 32 scenarios (two routes, four winds, both riding styles, with and without a forecast) gave the same results for the reversed route as the earlier calculation of the other direction, to within about 1 % of the route length. Those browser-level checks are done outside the repository. The source code is public at github.com/ubrowz/tailwind-tally, so all of the above can be inspected.

If you find a case where a number looks wrong, please say so on the issue tracker with the speed and wind you used.
