1. The short version

Tailwind Tally takes a GPX route and answers four questions:

  • How much of the route is with me or against me? Every 25 metre stretch is a tailwind, headwind or crosswind, for the route as recorded and in reverse, with a rough effort figure (sections 3 and 4). The wind is one steady value you set, or a forecast that changes with the clock and the place (sections 2 and 5).
  • How long will it take? At a steady speed the ride time is simply distance over speed. If you ride at steady power instead, your speed drops in a headwind and rises with a tailwind, and the app works out the resulting ride time on a flat road (section 6).
  • How will it feel? The Temperature tab estimates the feels-like temperature on every stretch (sections 8 to 12).
  • Where are the hills? The Climbs tab finds the climbs, numbers them and colours them by steepness, using elevation looked up on request (section 7).

The rest of this section is about the temperature. A thermometer tells you the air temperature. It does not tell you how the ride will feel. Riding into a headwind at 25 km/h on a 5 °C day feels much colder than 5 °C, and riding in humid heat feels hotter than the number says. Tailwind Tally estimates that feeling for every 25 metre stretch of your route:

  1. It works out the wind on you on that stretch: your own speed combined with the wind, given the direction you are riding.
  2. It takes the air temperature and humidity you enter, or the forecast for the moment you would be there.
  3. Below about 10 °C it uses the official wind chill formula. From 20 °C up it uses the apparent temperature, which includes wind and humidity. In between it fades smoothly from one to the other.
  4. It compares the result with the air temperature and colours the route by how much colder (blue) or hotter (red) each stretch feels.

It is a guide, not a measurement. Both formulas were built for a standard person in standard conditions. Clothing, sun, effort and the individual all matter, and none of them are in the model. Each section says exactly what is and is not included.

2. What goes in

  • Air temperature and humidity: typed in by you, or taken from the forecast.
  • Your speed: the cruising speed you enter, held constant (or, with steady power, your speed on a calm, flat day, plus your weight and the drag and rolling resistance of you and your bike under Advanced; section 6). The app does not model hills, stops or descents.
  • The ride’s start, in forecast mode: the date and the start time. Everything else about timing follows from the route and your speed.
  • The wind: direction and speed, typed in (speed as a Beaufort force) or taken from the forecast.
  • The route: your GPX track, resampled to 25 metre stretches, each with a heading.
  • Elevation, for the Climbs tab only: looked up online when you ask for it (section 7). The elevation in your GPX file is not used.

What leaves your device

Your GPX file is read in your browser and never uploaded. Two optional features contact a service, and only when you ask: the forecast sends up to three places from the route, each rounded to about 11 km, and the date; the elevation lookup for the Climbs tab sends points along the route, about 100 m apart and rounded to about 10 m, which is in effect the shape of the route. Both go straight to Open-Meteo. The map tiles are requested as you look at the map. The privacy policy lists all of it.

The forecast

In forecast mode the app asks Open-Meteo for hourly values, for the date you pick. It sends up to three places from your route (the start, the middle and the end), each rounded to 0.1° (about 11 km), never the route itself. You give only a start time. The ride then lasts as long as the route takes at your cruising speed, and each stretch gets its own clock time:

time at a stretch = start time + distance ridden so far / your speed

Temperature and humidity are read from the hourly forecast at that moment (linearly between the hours), and between the three places (linearly by distance along the route). Riding the route in reverse starts at the other end at the same clock time, so the same place is reached at a different moment and the two directions can differ.

The wind follows the clock and the place in the same way (section 5). The forecast wind is the standard 10 metre wind, which is also how the Beaufort scale is defined, so manual and forecast wind are directly comparable. If you type the wind yourself it is one steady value for the whole ride.

3. The wind on you

What cools or heats you is not the wind alone but the air moving past you, which is your own motion combined with the wind. On each 25 metre stretch, with v your speed, w the wind speed and φ the angle between the direction you ride and the direction the wind blows toward:

airspeed² = v² + w² − 2 · v · w · cos φ cos φ = +1 pure tailwind airspeed = v − w cos φ = 0 pure crosswind airspeed = √(v² + w²) cos φ = −1 pure headwind airspeed = v + w

Example. You ride at 25 km/h in a Beaufort 4 wind (24 km/h). Straight into it the air passes you at 49 km/h, across it at about 35 km/h, and with it at just 1 km/h. That difference is why the same route feels so different from one stretch to the next.

4. Tailwind, headwind and effort

With the wind on each stretch known (section 3), the app answers two questions: how much of the route is with you or against you, and how much harder the wind makes the ride.

Tailwind, headwind, crosswind

Each stretch is classified by the angle between your heading and the direction the wind blows toward. If the wind pushes along your direction of travel (cos φ > 0) the stretch is easy; if it pushes against you (cos φ < 0) it is hard. A narrow band around exact crosswind, 10° wide by default, counts as neither (neutral). The route map splits the two sides once more: stretches within 20° of dead-on (a 40° band by default) count as full tailwind or headwind. These are plain distance counts: a metre of gentle tailwind counts the same as a metre of gale.

Effort

Air resistance grows with the square of the airspeed, not in proportion to the wind. On a stretch the drag effort is proportional to the airspeed squared (the formula in section 3). The effort estimate is the distance-weighted average of that over the route, divided by the value for riding the same route at the same speed on a calm day. So 1.0× means the same as a calm day and 1.6× means 60 % more aerodynamic effort.

Because of the square, a headwind costs more than an equal tailwind saves. At 25 km/h in a 20 km/h wind, the airspeed is 45 km/h against (3.24× the calm effort) and 5 km/h with (0.04×). A route that is half headwind and half tailwind therefore averages 1.64×, more than a calm day. The estimate counts drag only: not rolling resistance, gradient or changes of speed, and it assumes you hold your cruising speed whatever the wind. If you ride at steady power, the effort figure is replaced by ride times (section 6).

Forward, reverse and the last half

The app shows the route as recorded and ridden in reverse. The last 50 % by distance is shown separately, since it is often the part that decides how a ride feels. With one steady wind, riding in reverse is the exact mirror image (every cos φ changes sign), so the app can check its own arithmetic: the tailwind kilometres one way must equal the headwind kilometres the other way (apart from the neutral band). The wind-direction sweep chart tries every steady wind direction from 0° to 359° and shows the tailwind share for each; it is a what-if for a steady wind and does not use the forecast.

5. A wind that changes during the ride

In forecast mode the wind follows the clock and the place exactly as the temperature does (section 2). Each stretch is judged against the wind expected when you would be there, and riding in reverse meets the same places at other moments. Between the hourly values, wind speed changes in a straight line and wind direction turns along the shortest arc: 350° to 10° passes through north, not through south. (Interpolating the east and north components instead would make the wind seem to weaken whenever it turns.) Between the places along the route the same rule applies.

What follows from it. The tailwind and headwind classes, the effort estimate and the feels-like temperature all use the wind on each stretch. What stops applying is the mirror image: with a changing wind, riding in reverse is not simply forward with the signs flipped, because it meets the wind at other moments. The identity and balance checks of section 4 assume one steady wind, so the app shows them as not applicable. Forward and reverse are calculated separately, each with its own timeline.

The average. The compass and the Beaufort field show the ride’s average: the mean speed, and a speed-weighted vector mean direction. If the wind changes so much that the average cancels itself out (west for an hour, then east), no single direction describes the ride, and the app says so instead of showing an invented one. Typing or clicking a wind by hand replaces the forecast wind with your steady value.

How much it matters. Real forecasts vary. For a Dutch location over 65 days in summer, the wind direction turned by more than 30° during a four-hour window on 28 % of days and by more than 45° on 14 %, though the biggest turns tended to come at light winds. On the 25.7 km sample loop, on a typical day with the wind turning 8° an hour, the time-aware answer differs from a single average wind by no more than about 2 percentage points of tailwind. When a front passes (wind turning 35° an hour, ridden at 10 km/h for 2.6 hours), the difference is 6 to 14 points, enough to change the advice: one average wind says riding in reverse is easier by 4.8 points, while the wind at the moment says riding forward is easier by 3.7. So it is a modest refinement on most days and a real one on days when the weather changes, and on long rides.

6. Riding at steady power

By default the app assumes you hold a steady speed. Some riders hold a steady effort instead: their power stays constant, so their speed drops in a headwind and rises with a tailwind. Choose Steady power under Riding style to model that. The speed you enter then means “the speed at this effort on a calm, flat day”, so you do not need to know your watts: the app works out the matching power and shows it.

The power balance

On a flat road, the power at the pedals balances air resistance and rolling resistance:

P · η = ( ½ · ρ · CdA · |vair| · (v − walong) + Crr · m · g ) · v

v is your speed, walong the wind component along your direction of travel (a tailwind is positive), and |vair| the size of the air velocity relative to you, so a crosswind adds drag through its size. ρ is the air density, CdA your drag area, Crr the rolling resistance, m the mass of rider and bike, g gravity and η the drivetrain efficiency. The defaults are 85 kg, CdA 0.32 m² (roughly hands on the hoods), Crr 0.005 and η 0.975; you can change them under Advanced. The air density comes from the air temperature at each stretch (dry air at sea-level pressure), so cold, dense air costs a little more.

Calibration

For your entered speed on a calm day, the balance gives the power. At 25 km/h and 20 °C the air resistance takes about 64 W and rolling resistance about 29 W at the wheel, so roughly 96 W at the pedals (about 99 W at 8 °C, where the air is denser). That is the steady power for the whole ride.

Solving for the speed, stretch by stretch

On each 25 metre stretch the app finds the speed at which that power exactly balances the resistance in the wind on that stretch. The speed on a stretch depends on the wind when you get there, and that moment depends on how fast you rode the stretches before it, so the ride is worked out stretch by stretch with the clock running at your real speed. Forward and reverse are worked out separately. In a tailwind the wind itself pushes the bike, so the search for the balancing speed starts beyond the point where the wind alone would carry you.

Holding the same power in a steady wind from the south-west it takes 63.6 minutes at 10 km/h, 69.1 at 20 km/h (average 22.4 km/h, ranging from 15 to 38 km/h along the loop) and 77.9 at 30 km/h.

What it changes in the results

  • The tailwind and headwind classes depend only on direction, so with a steady wind they are the same as at a steady speed.
  • The effort multiple (section 4) assumes a constant speed, so it is replaced by ride time: as recorded, in reverse and on a calm day, with the average, slowest and fastest speeds. It also shows the share of the time spent in a headwind, which differs from the share of the distance because you are slower there. In a Beaufort 4 wind the sample loop, ridden as recorded, has 67 % of the time but only 52 % of the distance into a headwind.
  • The feels-like temperature uses your real speed on each stretch, so a headwind chills you less than it would if you kept your speed up.
  • With a forecast, temperature, humidity and wind are read at the moment you really reach each stretch. The average wind shown in the compass fields is found with the calm-day timing, which is close but not exact.

Limits

  • Flat road only. On real routes hills dominate speed, and a rider holding constant power slows far more on a climb than in any wind. Elevation is not used in this calculation (the Climbs tab, section 7, shows the hills but does not feed the speed), so this shows what the wind does, not the whole ride.
  • Constant power is an idealisation. Many riders hold a perceived effort and vary it with terrain, traffic and fatigue.
  • Not modelled: drafting, cornering, braking, coasting downhill, a changing drag area with position or wind angle, and a changing efficiency. Air density ignores humidity and altitude, which change it by a few per cent.
  • The result depends on the rider parameters. The defaults are typical, not yours.

7. Climbs

The Climbs tab finds the hills in your route, numbers them and colours them by how steep they get. It is separate from the wind and temperature calculations: it uses only the elevation along the route, and it does not change any speed, time or temperature.

What you see

  • The map: each climb is a thick coloured stretch with its number beside it; the rest of the route is a thin grey line.
  • A table in the order you ride them: where each starts (km), its length, its gain, its average slope and its steepest 100 m.
  • A summary with the total ascent and descent, the number of climbs and the total climbing in them.
  • A choice between as recorded and in reverse. Riding the route backwards turns its descents into climbs, so the two directions can have different climbs, and the summary says how many the other direction has. Both come from the same elevation profile, so their ascent and descent totals are the same numbers swapped.

Where the elevation comes from

Always from one place: a lookup you ask for with the button Look up elevation online. Only when you tap it, the app sends points along the route (about one every 100 m, at most 400, coordinates rounded to about 10 m) to Open-Meteo. It answers with the ground height from the Copernicus GLO-90 terrain model, which has one value per 90 m. The service is free but limits how many points one address may ask for, which is why a very long route gets a wider spacing.

The elevation recorded in a GPX file is not used, even when it is there. Files differ a lot in how good their elevation is (a barometer, a phone’s GPS, a map service), and using it for some routes and not others made the results hard to compare. One source means every route, and both directions of it, are measured the same way. The price is that the shape of the route leaves your device when you use this tab, and that the source has limits:

  • A 90 m model flattens ramps shorter than about 100 m.
  • It shows the land as mapped, not the road: bridges, tunnels, viaducts and cuttings are not in it, and in forest or built-up areas it may show the treetops or roofs.
  • Where a climb is close to the average-slope threshold, small differences in the data can decide whether it is listed. On a test route (a straight line through South Limburg) the lookup gave five of the six climbs found from a file made from the same model, and the sixth sat at 1.99 % against the 2 % limit.
  • On a real hilly ride of 87 km the lookup found 10 climbs where the file’s own elevation gave 11, with almost the same positions and gains.

Why it needs smoothing

The terrain model is noisy: it wobbles by metres from point to point, and it is a surface model, so it also includes treetops and roofs. Added up naively, that looks like climbing. So the profile is treated before anything is measured:

  1. The profile is put on an even grid of one point per 25 m of route, by interpolation.
  2. A median of three points removes single spikes, and a moving average over about 150 m smooths the rest.
  3. Highs and lows are then found with a 3 m dead-band: a rise or fall counts only once it exceeds 3 m from the last extreme. The total ascent and descent shown use the same rule.

What counts as a climb

A climb runs from a low to the next high. Two climbs separated by a short dip (less than a quarter of the gain, within 400 m) are merged into one, as riders experience them. Each climb ends at the first point within 1 m of its top and starts at the last point within 1 m of its bottom, so a flat top does not stretch it. Then two thresholds, both adjustable, decide what is shown:

  • Minimum gain, 25 m by default. Raise it to see only the big ones.
  • Minimum average slope, 2 % by default. This keeps long, barely rising stretches, which are mostly noise or false flats, from being reported as climbs.

The colours

Each climb is coloured by its maximum slope: the steepest 100 m stretch of the smoothed profile. There are five classes: under 5 %, 5–8 %, 8–12 %, 12–16 % and 16 % and up. It is one violet hue in five steps, standing out more the steeper the climb, with the rest of the route drawn thin and grey. The choice of forward or reverse works by running the same detection on the reversed profile, so a climb ridden one way is a descent the other way.

Limits

  • Trees and buildings. On a flat ride through woods and towns the model reads 5 to 10 m too high in patches. On one flat 78 km ride the total ascent came out at 307 m against 76 m in Strava’s own elevation, so treat the ascent and descent totals of a flat route with care. Real hills stand out from such patches, but small climbs near the minimum may be missed or invented.
  • The maximum slope depends on the smoothing. A short, sharp ramp of 100 m or less is flattened by smoothing over 150 m, so the steepest figure is a lower bound for such ramps. That is why the average is shown next to it: the average is much less sensitive.
  • The thresholds are conventions. There is no official definition of a climb. Change them if a hill you know is missing or a minor one is shown.
  • Accuracy is the terrain model’s. The app cannot see the road itself, only the land as mapped at 90 m.
  • Not used elsewhere. Hills do not change the wind classes, the ride time at steady power or the feels-like temperature. There is no elevation chart, because the profile of a surface model is rougher than the ones you know from Strava or a bike computer.

8. Cold: wind chill

For cold weather the app uses the standard wind chill formula that Environment Canada and the US National Weather Service have used since 2001. It comes from Osczevski and Bluestein (2005), who based it on a model of heat flow from the upwind side of a head-sized cylinder moving at walking speed into the wind. With T in °C and V the airspeed in km/h:

wind chill = 13.12 + 0.6215 T − 11.37 V0.16 + 0.3965 T V0.16

It is officially defined only for T ≤ 10 °C and V > 4.8 km/h (the 3 mph limit in the US version). Below 4.8 km/h of airspeed there is no wind chill, which is why a tailwind that matches your speed brings no cooling at all.

Example. At −10 °C it feels like −15.3 °C at 10 km/h of airspeed, −18.8 °C at 25 km/h and −21.3 °C at 45 km/h.

Caveats. The formula describes how fast exposed skin on the face cools, in steady conditions, for someone walking. Using a cyclist’s airspeed is the usual approximation and not what it was calibrated for. The authors themselves point out the steady-state character of the concept.

9. Mild and hot: apparent temperature

Wind chill is not defined for mild weather, and it ignores humidity. From 20 °C up the app uses the apparent temperature of Robert Steadman, in the form used by the Australian Bureau of Meteorology. It combines temperature, humidity and wind. With T in °C, RH the relative humidity in % and v the wind in metres per second:

apparent temperature = T + 0.33 · e − 0.70 · v − 4.00 e = (RH / 100) · 6.105 · exp( 17.27 T / (237.7 + T) ) [hPa, water vapour pressure]

It reads as three effects on top of the air temperature: humid air feels warmer (the 0.33·e term, because sweat evaporates more slowly), moving air feels cooler (0.7 °C for every metre per second), and a fixed offset of −4 °C. The model is of an adult walking outdoors in the shade. In the original formula v is the wind at 10 metres; the app uses the airspeed on the rider, the same approximation as for the wind chill.

Example. At 32 °C and 70 % humidity the water vapour pressure is 33.2 hPa, so humidity adds 10.9 °C. Riding at 25 km/h (6.9 m/s) takes 4.9 °C off, and the offset another 4.0. The result is 34.1 °C, only 2 °C above the air temperature. In still air the same day would feel like 38.9 °C.

Riding speed (no other wind)Feels like at 32 °C, 70 %

Steadman’s own analysis found that, for normal midday climates in the US, humidity, wind and sun move the apparent temperature by between −5 and +7 K from the dry-bulb temperature. That is the scale of effect to expect.

10. Putting the two together

The two models are not the same physics, and they disagree by a few degrees in the middle, so switching between them at a single temperature would make the result jump. Instead the app fades from one to the other between 10 °C (where the official wind chill ends) and 20 °C:

x = (T − 10) / 10 (limited to 0 … 1) weight = 3x² − 2x³ (a smooth S-curve) feels like = (1 − weight) · wind chill + weight · apparent temperature

Above 10 °C the wind chill formula is evaluated beyond its official range, and capped so it never says it feels warmer than the air. That extension is only used inside the fade zone, and it is a plausible shape, not something that has been validated. The result at 15 °C, 60 % humidity and 25 km/h: wind chill 13.4 °C, apparent temperature 9.5 °C, weight 0.5, so 11.4 °C.

Note the small dip in the cooling between 10 and 20 °C. It is the seam between the two models, not a real effect. The absolute feels-like temperature still rises steadily with the air temperature.

A known limit. In the fade zone, in very dry air (20 % humidity or less) at high airspeed (40 km/h or more), a slightly warmer air temperature can feel up to 1 °C colder, and up to about 3 °C in the extreme corner of bone-dry air at 60 km/h. It never happens up to 25 km/h of airspeed or in humid air.

11. Standing still: the heat index

The apparent temperature includes the cooling from riding. If you stop, or crawl up a steep hill with no breeze, that cooling disappears. For that case the app shows the heat index of the US National Weather Service, which uses only temperature and humidity. It comes from Steadman’s work and Rothfusz’s regression fit (1990): a simple formula for milder conditions, and a longer regression once the result reaches 80 °F (about 27 °C), with two extra adjustments for very dry or very humid air. The regression is published at the National Weather Service; the page linked under Sources lists every coefficient.

The published algorithm has small steps where it switches between its parts (up to about 1.3 °C in humid air), the same kind of jump the wind chill cutoff would cause. The app smooths them: it blends the simple formula into the regression over 78–82 °F, fades the two humidity adjustments in over 78–80 °F, and ramps the whole effect in between 22 and 26 °C. From about 29 °C upwards the result is exactly the published value.

Example. At 32 °C and 70 % humidity the heat index is 40.4 °C, 8.4 °C above the air temperature and far above the 34.1 °C you would feel riding at 25 km/h.

The heat index assumes shade, and the National Weather Service notes that direct sunlight can add up to 15 °F (about 8 °C). It also carries the Weather Service’s heat-illness categories, which the app quotes when the standing-still value is high: from 103 °F (39 °C) heat cramps or heat exhaustion are likely and heat stroke is possible, and from 125 °F (52 °C) heat stroke is highly likely.

Each colour arm is one hue running from light to dark, so a stronger effect is always more intense: darker in the light theme, lighter in the dark theme, so it always stands out against the map. The “coldest tenth” and “hottest tenth” figures are the average feels-like over the coldest or hottest 10 % of the distance, not the single most extreme stretch, so one stray GPS point cannot set the headline number.

13. What this is not

  • Not a measurement. It is a model of an average person. Individuals differ a lot, and what a cyclist feels is subjective.
  • No sun. Neither formula includes solar radiation. Full sun feels hotter (the heat index notes up to about 8 °C).
  • No clothing, no effort. The models do not know what you wear or how hard you work. Riding hard generates heat the models do not count.
  • Constant speed, or constant power on a flat road. The clock and the airspeed use your cruising speed, or with steady power the speed that power gives on each stretch. Climbs, descents, stops and sprints are not modelled (the Climbs tab shows the hills but does not feed the speed), so timing is approximate. The forecast is hourly, so that is usually accurate enough.
  • Wind at 10 metres, hourly. The forecast wind is the standard 10 metre wind, and the wind at a rider’s height is lower, especially among trees and buildings. Gusts are not modelled. Between the hourly values the wind is interpolated, so a sudden shift within an hour is smoothed.
  • Forecast resolution. Weather models resolve places a few kilometres apart at best, and three sample places are used. Local effects such as valleys, coasts, buildings and tree cover are not captured.
  • Very hot, dry air. Above about 35 °C in dry air, moving air stops helping and can add heat. The apparent temperature formula does not capture that.
  • Not medical or safety advice. The heat-illness categories quoted are the National Weather Service’s for the standing-still heat index. Use your own judgement and local guidance.

14. How it is checked

The maths is covered by automated tests (in the tests folder of the repository, run with node tests/pure.test.js) that run against the code in this app itself: it is cut out of the page and run as it is. They include: the wind chill and heat index reproduce values published by the National Weather Service (for example 0 °F with a 15 mph wind gives −19 °F), and agree with an independent copy of the published Fahrenheit and mph formula across a grid of temperatures and speeds; the heat index equals the published algorithm from about 29 °C up, while the published algorithm is shown to have steps that the app’s version does not; the combined result has no jump anywhere; and faster air always feels cooler. For the wind: directions turn along the shortest arc, a forecast wind that does not change gives the same answer as a steady one, and a wind that reverses halfway along a route is judged stretch by stretch. For steady power: the speed solver returns your entered speed on a calm day, satisfies the power balance exactly on every stretch (headwind, tailwind and crosswind), and matches an independent calculation on the sample loop, and the clock follows the rider’s real speed. For climbs: slope classes at their edges, an exact 5 % climb, noise of ±2 m on a 40 km flat road giving no climbs, a noisy real climb still being found, reverse riding, the number of a climb landing on the right stretch of the route, and a real stretch of South Limburg terrain giving the climbs expected from its raw profile. For the lookup: the points sent are evenly spaced, include the first and last point of the route, come in batches within the service’s limit and are rounded as stated above. The lookup was also compared with the live service on the South Limburg route and on two long rides (one flat, one hilly); the flat one showed the treetop effect described in section 7. The steady-wind results were also compared with a stored set of 60 scenarios before and after the changing-wind work, and were identical. Those browser-level checks are done outside the repository. The source code is public at github.com/ubrowz/tailwind-tally, so all of the above can be inspected.

If you find a case where a number looks wrong, please say so on the issue tracker with the temperature, humidity, speed and wind you used.
